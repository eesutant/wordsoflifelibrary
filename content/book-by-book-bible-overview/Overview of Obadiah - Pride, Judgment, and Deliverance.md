## Opening Story  
  
She had never read Obadiah. Perhaps you haven't either. It is easy to miss, a single page tucked quietly between Amos and Jonah, a book so small you could flip past it without noticing. Most readers never wander into this corner of Scripture. It feels obscure, distant, and, if we're honest, irrelevant.  
  
But one afternoon, after a difficult conversation with a close friend, she found herself thinking about the strange ways people hurt each other. Not the dramatic betrayals, but the subtle ones: the cold silence that creeps into a relationship, the quiet satisfaction when someone who wounded you finally stumbles, the indifference that settles in when you feel tired of caring, the small ways you take advantage of someone's weakness without meaning to.  
  
Perhaps you know those moments too. Moments when you realise something inside you is not as gentle as you thought. Something self‑protective. Something defensive. Something proud.  
  
She didn't have words for it, but she sensed a conflict inside her, a tug‑of‑war between who she wanted to be and who she was becoming. That evening she opened her Bible, not knowing where to turn, and her eyes fell on a heading she had never noticed: **Obadiah.**  
  
She almost skipped it. But something made her pause. She began reading and discovered this tiny book spoke directly to the conflict inside her. It was not about ancient geography or forgotten nations. It was about her heart. And perhaps it is about yours too.  
  
## Introduction: Two Nations, Two Brothers, Two Natures  
  
Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, a single page, easily missed, easily dismissed. On the surface, it appears to be nothing more than a pronouncement of doom against an ancient, long‑forgotten nation called **Edom**. But Scripture has a beautiful way of hiding treasure beneath simplicity. What looks small often contains something vast. What seems obscure often reveals something essential. And Obadiah is one of those books: tiny in size, immense in significance.  
  
We know almost nothing about the prophet himself. His name, _Obadiah_, means **"servant of Yahweh."** And true to his name, he appears briefly, speaks faithfully, and disappears. He does not tell us his story. He does not describe his background. He does not explain his calling. He simply delivers God's message and fades into the background. Most scholars believe he lived around the time of **Jeremiah**, during or shortly after the fall of Jerusalem, a time of national trauma, spiritual crisis, and deep reflection.  
  
But while Obadiah tells us little about the man, he tells us much about the **message**, a message that reaches far beyond Edom, far beyond ancient history, and far beyond geopolitical conflict. It reaches into the heart of every reader.  
  
On the surface, Obadiah is about **two nations**:  
  
- **Israel**, the people of Jacob  
- **Edom**, the people of Esau  
  
These nations were neighbours, but they were never friends. Their hostility began long before borders existed. It began in a womb.  
  
Behind the nations stand **two brothers**, Jacob and Esau, whose story stretches from Genesis to Malachi. Even before birth, they struggled. Their lives were marked by rivalry, deception, resentment, and blessing. And their descendants inherited the same antagonism. Israel and Edom clashed repeatedly: during the Exodus, during the monarchy, during the exile. From beginning to end, Scripture portrays them as locked in perpetual conflict.  
  
But Obadiah reveals something deeper: These two men, Jacob and Esau, are not just historical figures. They are **symbols**. They are **pictures**. They are **interpretational constants** woven through the entire Bible.  
  
Jacob represents the **spirit**; the new nature God creates within His people. Esau represents the **flesh**; the old nature that resists God.  
  
This is why God says: "I have loved Jacob, but Esau I have hated." (Malachi 1:2 NIV) Not because God hates people, but because He hates the principle that destroys them.  
  
Throughout Scripture, God uses pictures to help us understand spiritual truth. Oil consistently symbolises the Holy Spirit. Wine symbolises joy. Leaven symbolises evil. And Jacob and Esau symbolise the **inner conflict** that every believer experiences, the war between the flesh and the Spirit.  
  
The New Testament makes this explicit: "For the flesh desires what is contrary to the Spirit, and the Spirit what is contrary to the flesh." (Galatians 5:17 NIV)  
  
This is why Obadiah matters. This is why this tiny book speaks so powerfully. This is why it belongs not only to ancient Israel but to you.  
  
Obadiah is not merely about Edom's downfall. It is about the **Edom within you**, the pride, self‑sufficiency, indifference, and resistance to God that still lives in your heart.  
  
And it is about the **Jacob within you**, the Spirit‑born desire for holiness, humility, and transformation.  
  
Obadiah is the story of two nations, two brothers, and two natures. It is the story of the conflict inside every believer. It is the story of the flesh and the Spirit. It is the story of you.  
  
### 1. The Pride of Edom: The Nature God Opposes 
  
Obadiah begins by turning the spotlight directly onto **Esau**, the man of the flesh, and onto **Edom**, the proud nation that embodies that fleshly nature. The prophet answers a question that echoes through Scripture: _Why does God oppose Esau?_ Why does God set Himself against the flesh?  
  
Obadiah answers plainly: "The pride of your heart has deceived you… you who say to yourself, Who can bring me down to the ground?" (Obadiah 1:3 NIV)  
  
Pride is the root of all human evil. Pride is the essence of the flesh. Pride is the inward twist that resists God, defies His purposes, and elevates self.  
  
Scripture consistently identifies pride as the foundational sin. Proverbs lists it first among the things the Lord hates. Pride is the satanic distortion inherited by every child of Adam, the independent ego that evaluates everything in terms of its benefit or threat to self. Pride makes the self the centre of the universe, the rival god within the human heart.  
  
Obadiah shows how pride expresses itself in Edom, and how it expresses itself in you.  
  
#### 1. Pride appears as self‑sufficiency. 
  
Edom lived in Petra; a fortress carved into towering cliffs. Its natural defences made the nation feel untouchable. They believed no one could reach them, no one could threaten them, no one could bring them down.  
  
But God says: "Though you soar like the eagle… from there I will bring you down." (Obadiah 1:4 NIV)  
  
Self‑sufficiency is the voice that says, "I don't need God here. I can run my life myself."  
  
It appears in the person who believes they can make their own decisions without God's wisdom. It appears in the Christian who prays on Sunday but relies entirely on self on Monday. It appears in the subtle assumption that competence replaces dependence.  
  
Jesus lived in utter dependence on the Father. Wherever you feel capable without God, the flesh is at work.  
  
#### 2. Pride appears as violence.  
  
Obadiah says: "Because of the violence against your brother Jacob…" (Obadiah 1:10 NIV)  
  
Violence is not only physical harm. It is harshness, cruelty, domination, emotional force. It is the unbroken ego that lashes out when self is threatened.  
  
Violence is pride defending its throne.  
  
#### 3. Pride appears as indifference.  
  
Obadiah rebukes Edom for standing aloof while Jerusalem suffered: "You stood aloof while strangers carried off his wealth…" (Obadiah 1:11 NIV)  
  
Indifference is pride in its quietest form. It is the coldness that refuses to care. It is the relational distance that says, "Your pain is not my concern."  
  
Indifference destroys marriages, friendships, and communities. It is Esau at work.  
  
#### 4. Pride appears as gloating.  
  
Obadiah says: "You should not gloat over your brother in the day of his misfortune…" (Obadiah 1:12 NIV)  
  
Gloating is the secret delight in another's failure. It is the inward smirk that says, "You had it coming." It is the satisfaction of being proven right. It is pride celebrating someone else's pain.  
  
#### 5. Pride appears as exploitation.  
  
Edom took advantage of Israel's weakness: "You should not wait at the crossroads to cut down their fugitives…" (Obadiah 1:14 NIV)  
  
Exploitation is using someone's vulnerability for your benefit. It is taking advantage of a mistake, a weakness, a misfortune. It is pride turning another's distress into personal gain.  
  
#### The tragedy of pride is blindness.  
  
Obadiah repeats the warning: "The pride of your heart has deceived you." (Obadiah 1:3 NIV)  
  
Pride blinds you to pride. You do not see it until it has already begun to destroy you.  
  
Edom trusted its cliffs, its alliances, its strength. But God says their allies will betray them, their friends will trap them, their security will collapse: "All your allies will force you to the border; your friends will deceive and overpower you." (Obadiah 1:7 NIV)  
  
Pride always ends this way. It traps you. It tricks you. It trips you. It leaves you exposed.  
  
It is the emperor walking naked through the streets, convinced he is clothed in glory.  
  
Obadiah wants you to see this: **Esau is not merely an ancient figure. Esau is alive in you.** The flesh is real. Pride is real. Self‑sufficiency is real. Indifference is real. Gloating is real. Exploitation is real. And unless God intervenes, pride will deceive you until it destroys you.  
  
### 2. The Judgment of Edom: God's Ruthless Opposition to the Flesh  
  
Once Obadiah exposes the nature of pride, he immediately turns to its destiny. The prophet does not leave the reader wondering what becomes of Esau, the flesh, or the proud nation of Edom. He speaks with sobering clarity: "The day of the Lord is near for all nations. As you have done, it will be done to you; your deeds will return upon your own head." (Obadiah 1:15 NIV)  
  
This is the turning point of the book. It is the moment when God declares His verdict on the flesh.  
  
**The day of the Lord is God's decisive confrontation with pride.**  
  
Edom had lived in self‑sufficiency, indifference, gloating, and exploitation. They had resisted God, harmed His people, and exalted themselves. But now God announces that their actions will boomerang back upon them. What they have sown, they will reap. What they have inflicted, they will experience.  
  
Obadiah continues: "Just as you drank on my holy hill, so all the nations will drink continually; they will drink and drink and be as if they had never been." (Obadiah 1:16 NIV)  
  
This imagery of drinking is a picture of judgment, the cup of God's wrath poured out on the proud. Edom will stagger under the consequences of its own arrogance. The nation that felt invincible will be reduced to nothing.  
  
**For Esau, there is no escape.**  
  
This is the sobering truth Obadiah wants the reader to grasp: God's judgment on the flesh is **inescapable**.  
  
Edom's downfall is not merely historical. It is theological. It is symbolic. It is personal.  
  
Edom represents the flesh, the Esau‑nature within you, and God declares that He will never make peace with it. He will never negotiate with pride. He will never tolerate self‑sufficiency. He will never allow the flesh to coexist peacefully with the Spirit.  
  
This is why Scripture says: "I have loved Jacob, but Esau I have hated." (Malachi 1:2 NIV)  
  
Not because God hates people, but because He hates the principle that destroys them.  
  
**God's war against the flesh is relentless.**  
  
Obadiah's message echoes a pattern seen throughout Scripture. One of Esau's descendants, **Amalek**, attacked Israel during the Exodus. God responded with a permanent decree:  
  
"I will completely blot out the name of Amalek from under heaven." (Exodus 17:14 NIV) "The Lord will be at war against the Amalekites from generation to generation." (Exodus 17:16 NIV)  
  
This is not merely about Amalek. It is about the flesh.  
  
God will wage war against the flesh **forever**. He will never compromise with it. He will never soften toward it. He will never allow it to rule His people.  
  
**Why is God so ruthless toward the flesh?**  
  
Because the flesh is ruthless toward you.  
  
The flesh destroys marriages. The flesh destroys friendships. The flesh destroys communities. The flesh destroys spiritual life. The flesh destroys the human soul.  
  
The flesh blinds you, deceives you, traps you, and ultimately ruins you.  
  
Obadiah shows this vividly: "All your allies will force you to the border; your friends will deceive and overpower you." (Obadiah 1:7 NIV)  
  
Pride always ends this way. It collapses under its own weight. It betrays itself. It leaves you exposed.  
  
**The judgment of Edom is the judgment of the flesh.**  
  
Edom trusted its cliffs. You may trust your competence.  
  
Edom trusted its alliances. You may trust your reputation.  
  
Edom trusted its strength. You may trust your independence.  
  
But God says: "I will bring you down." (Obadiah 1:4 NIV)  
  
Not to crush you, but to save you.  
  
The cross is God's decisive act of judgment against the flesh. It is the place where pride dies. It is the place where self‑sufficiency is exposed. It is the place where the Spirit begins His work of transformation.  
  
Obadiah wants you to see this clearly: **Esau cannot be reformed. He must be destroyed.** The flesh cannot be improved. It must be crucified. Pride cannot be managed. It must be killed.  
  
God's judgment on Edom is not merely historical. It is a picture of His relentless, loving, saving opposition to the flesh within you.  
  
### 3. The Triumph of Jacob: The Spirit's Victory in the Believer  
  
After exposing the pride of Edom and announcing God's unyielding judgment against the flesh, Obadiah turns to a radically different scene, one filled with hope, holiness, and triumph. The prophet shifts the spotlight from **Esau** to **Jacob**, from the flesh to the Spirit, from ruin to restoration.  
  
Obadiah writes: "But on Mount Zion will be deliverance; it will be holy, and Jacob will possess his inheritance." (Obadiah 1:17 NIV)  
  
This is the turning point of the book. It is the moment when the reader sees that judgment is not the final word. The flesh will fall, but the Spirit will rise.  
  
**Jacob's future is deliverance.**  
  
Mount Zion, the symbol of God's presence, God's people, and God's purposes , becomes the place of escape, holiness, and inheritance. Jacob, representing the Spirit‑born nature within the believer, will not only survive; he will **possess** what God intended for him all along.  
  
Obadiah continues with vivid imagery: "Jacob will be a fire and Joseph a flame; Esau will be stubble, and they will set him on fire and destroy him." (Obadiah 1:18 NIV)  
  
This is not cruelty. This is transformation.  
  
**The Spirit burns away the flesh.**  
  
Jacob becomes a fire, a picture of purity, power, and holiness. Esau becomes stubble, a picture of the flesh, dry, brittle, and easily consumed.  
  
The Spirit does not negotiate with the flesh. He destroys it.  
  
This is the ruthlessness of God, not toward you, but toward the sin that enslaves you. The Holy Spirit enters the human heart to wage war against pride, self‑sufficiency, indifference, gloating, and exploitation. He comes to burn away everything that resists God. He comes to bring Jacob, the Spirit, into full possession of his inheritance.  
  
And Obadiah ends with a breathtaking declaration: "Deliverers will go up on Mount Zion to govern the mountains of Esau. And the kingdom will be the Lord's." (Obadiah 1:21 NIV)  
  
This is the climax of the book. This is the goal of redemption. This is the future of every believer.  
  
**The kingdom will be the Lord's, including the kingdom of your life.**  
  
Obadiah is not merely describing geopolitical events. He is describing spiritual reality.  
  
Jacob and Esau appear again in the New Testament, this time in the persons of **Jesus** and **Herod**. Herod, an Edomite, stands before Jesus during the final week of the Lord's suffering. Herod mocks Him, questions Him, and treats Him with contempt. But Jesus remains silent. Why?  
  
Because the Spirit has nothing to say to the flesh. There can be no compromise. No negotiation. No middle ground.  
  
Herod represents Esau, proud, resistant, self‑sufficient. Jesus represents Jacob, humble, dependent, Spirit‑filled.  
  
And the outcome is exactly what Obadiah foretold: Herod ends in disgrace, exile, and death. Jesus goes to the cross, enters the grave, and rises as King.  
  
Two natures. Two destinies. Two futures.  
  
**And Obadiah asks you a question: Which one is ruling in you?**  
  
Are you living as a king, reigning with Christ, possessing your spiritual inheritance, letting the Spirit burn away the flesh?  
  
Or are you living as a prisoner, like Herod, imagining yourself free while bound by chains of pride, self‑sufficiency, and resistance to God?  
  
**The cross is the dividing line between Jacob and Esau.**  
  
The cross denies you any right to self‑sufficiency. The cross denies you any right to self‑advantage. The cross denies you any right to self‑exaltation. The cross denies you any right to indifference, gloating, exploitation, or self‑righteousness.  
  
The cross kills Esau. The cross frees Jacob. The cross makes the kingdom the Lord's.  
  
This is why Obadiah ends with such a triumphant declaration: "And the kingdom will be the Lord's." (Obadiah 1:21 NIV)  
  
This is not merely a statement about the end of history. It is a statement about the end of **your** history, the end of the flesh's rule, the end of pride's dominion, the end of self‑sufficiency's tyranny. It is the beginning of the Spirit's reign, the beginning of holiness, the beginning of freedom, the beginning of life.  
  
Obadiah is not simply describing what will happen to nations. He is describing what will happen to **you** when the Spirit triumphs.  
  
Jacob will possess his inheritance. Jacob will become a fire. Jacob will reign. Jacob will rise.  
  
Esau will be stubble. Esau will be consumed. Esau will fall. Esau will be no more.  
  
And the kingdom, the kingdom of your life, will belong to the Lord.  
  
## So What? Why Obadiah Matters Today  
  
Obadiah is ancient, but its message is startlingly modern. It speaks directly into the inner conflict you experience every day. It explains why spiritual life feels like a battle, why pride is so persistent, why humility is so difficult, and why transformation requires surrender.  
  
### 1. Obadiah reveals the true nature of pride and helps you see it in yourself. 
  
Pride is not merely arrogance. It is self‑sufficiency, indifference, gloating, exploitation, and blindness. It is the quiet voice that says, "I don't need God here."  
  
If you have ever felt that voice, Obadiah is speaking to you.  
  
### 2. Obadiah explains why you feel torn inside.  
  
The flesh and the Spirit are irreconcilably opposed. They cannot coexist peacefully. Every believer feels this tension.  
  
If you have ever wondered why obedience feels hard, why humility feels unnatural, why surrender feels costly, Obadiah is explaining your experience.  
  
### 3. Obadiah shows that God will never negotiate with the flesh.  
  
He will destroy it completely, through the cross, through discipline, through the Spirit's relentless work. God is ruthless toward the flesh because the flesh is ruthless toward you.  
  
If you have ever felt God confronting your pride, your self‑sufficiency, your defensiveness, He is doing what Obadiah describes.  
  
### 4. Obadiah assures you that the Spirit will win.  
  
Jacob will possess his inheritance. The Spirit will bring you into holiness, humility, and freedom. The flesh will not have the final word.  
  
If you have ever feared that you will never change, never grow, never overcome, Obadiah promises that the Spirit will triumph.  
  
### 5. Obadiah calls you to examine your heart honestly.  
  
Where is Esau ruling? Where is pride hiding? Where is self‑sufficiency masquerading as strength? Where is indifference posing as emotional distance?  
  
If you have ever sensed something in you resisting God, Obadiah invites you to name it.  
  
### 6. Obadiah invites you to surrender to the cross.  
  
To let the cross do its work. To let the Spirit burn away the flesh. To let God's kingdom rule within you.  
  
If you have ever longed for freedom, for humility, for gentleness, for transformation, Obadiah shows you the path.  
  
### 7. Obadiah teaches you that spiritual victory is not passive.  
  
The Spirit destroys the flesh, but you must cooperate, confessing pride, rejecting self‑sufficiency, choosing humility, embracing dependence.  
  
If you have ever wondered what your part is, Obadiah clarifies it.  
  
### 8. Obadiah promises that the kingdom of your life can belong entirely to God.  
  
Not partially. Not occasionally. Not symbolically. But truly.  
  
If you have ever longed for your life to be wholly God's, Obadiah assures you it is possible.  
  
## Closing Story: When Obadiah Becomes Your Mirror  
  
Months after reading Obadiah for the first time, she found herself returning to the book, not out of obligation, but out of recognition. Something in its pages had lodged itself in her heart. It was no longer a strange prophecy about a forgotten nation. It had become a mirror.  
  
Perhaps you know that feeling too. The sense that Scripture is reading you. Naming you. Revealing you.  
  
She began noticing subtle patterns in her life: the quiet satisfaction she felt when someone who hurt her stumbled, the indifference that crept into her marriage, the self‑sufficiency that made her prayer life thin, the small ways she took advantage of others without meaning to, the defensiveness that rose whenever she was challenged.  
  
Perhaps you have noticed similar patterns, moments when Esau seems very much alive in you. But she also realised something else: Jacob was alive in her too. The Spirit was at work, nudging, convicting, softening, reshaping. She felt moments of unexpected tenderness, glimpses of humility, a growing discomfort with pride, a desire to be free.  
  
Perhaps you have felt that too, the Spirit's quiet, persistent work. One evening she prayed, "Lord, destroy Esau in me. Let Your kingdom be Yours." It was not dramatic. It was not emotional. But it was real.  
  
And in the quiet that followed, she sensed something she had not felt in a long time: a gentle, steady, Spirit‑born assurance that the battle inside her was not hopeless. Jacob would rise. Esau would fall. The Spirit would win.  
  
As she closed her Bible, she whispered a truth Obadiah had taught her: **"The kingdom will be the Lord's."**  
  
And perhaps, as you finish this overview, you feel the same truth settling into your own heart, not as a distant prophecy, but as a personal promise.
