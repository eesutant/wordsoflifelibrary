Overview of Matthew Part 2 – Following the King to the Cross and the Crown 🟢 NEW

November 26, 2026·3033 words·15 mins

Adapted from insights by Ray C. Stedman and other biblical reflections.

Opening Story
#

Imagine finally finding the king you have been waiting for. For years, people have spoken about him. They have longed for the day he would come, defeat his enemies, and establish a kingdom marked by justice, peace, and righteousness. When he finally arrives, he exceeds every expectation. His wisdom astonishes the crowds, his authority commands attention, and there is little doubt that he is the king everyone has been hoping for. Then, just as confidence in him reaches its highest point, he begins speaking about suffering. Instead of describing victory, he speaks of rejection. Instead of preparing for a coronation, he predicts his own death.

His followers are bewildered. They know he is the king, but they cannot understand why his road leads toward sacrifice rather than triumph. The problem is not that they have identified the wrong king. The problem is that they have misunderstood the kind of king he has come to be. Many people encounter Jesus in much the same way. We admire His teaching, are moved by His compassion, and find comfort in His promises. Yet we often struggle when He challenges our assumptions about power, greatness, success, and even discipleship itself. Like the disciples, we are often prepared to follow a King who fulfills our expectations, but we are less prepared for a King who transforms them.

That is where the second half of Matthew’s Gospel begins. Peter has already confessed, “You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.” (Matthew 16:16 NIV) The question of Jesus’ identity has largely been answered. What remains is to discover why He came, how He would accomplish His mission, and what it truly means to follow Him.

The journey ahead will lead through Jerusalem, the cross, and the empty tomb. By the end, the disciples will discover that God’s greatest victory was won in a way none of them expected, and readers will be confronted with the same question that has echoed throughout the Gospel: not merely whether Jesus is King, but whether we are willing to let Him reign.

Introduction to the Second Half of Matthew
#

The confession at Caesarea Philippi forms one of the most important turning points in the entire Gospel. For sixteen chapters, Matthew has been answering the question of Jesus’ identity. Through carefully selected events, teachings, and miracles, he has shown his readers that Jesus is the promised Messiah, the Son of David, the Son of God, and Immanuel, “God with us.” The evidence has accumulated steadily until Peter speaks on behalf of the disciples and declares what Matthew has wanted readers to recognize all along: Jesus is God’s promised King.

At that moment, however, the direction of the Gospel changes dramatically. Matthew records a statement that becomes the key to everything that follows: “From that time on Jesus began to explain to his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer many things at the hands of the elders, the chief priests and the teachers of the law, and that he must be killed and on the third day be raised to life.” (Matthew 16:21 NIV)

The importance of this transition cannot be overstated. Until now, the question has been whether Jesus is the Messiah. From this point onward, the question becomes what kind of Messiah He is.

For the disciples, this revelation is deeply unsettling. They expected God’s King to establish His kingdom through visible triumph and power. Instead, Jesus speaks about suffering, sacrifice, and death. Yet Matthew wants his readers to understand that the cross is not a departure from God’s plan but the very means by which that plan will be fulfilled.

As Jesus journeys toward Jerusalem, He prepares His disciples for this reality. Through His teaching, parables, and personal example, He reveals a kingdom radically different from the kingdoms of this world. True greatness is found in humility, leadership is expressed through service, and victory comes through self-giving love. The King who came to save His people will accomplish His mission not by defeating others, but by giving Himself for them.

For modern readers, the challenge remains the same. It is possible to recognize Jesus as King while still misunderstanding His ways. Matthew therefore invites us to follow the disciples on their journey from recognition to understanding, as they discover that God’s greatest victory would come through a cross before it would come through a crown.

The King’s Road to Jerusalem (Matthew 16:21-20:34)
#

Following Peter’s confession at Caesarea Philippi, Jesus begins revealing the true nature of His mission. For the first time, He openly tells His disciples that He must go to Jerusalem, suffer at the hands of His enemies, be killed, and rise again. The revelation is shocking. Although the disciples have recognized Jesus as the Messiah, they still expect a King who will establish His kingdom through visible power and victory. They are not prepared for a Messiah whose path leads to a cross.

Yet Matthew shows that God’s purposes often overturn human expectations. The Kingdom Jesus introduces is radically different from the kingdoms of this world. Greatness is found in humility, leadership is expressed through service, and true life is discovered through self-denial. When Jesus declares, “Whoever wants to be my disciple must deny themselves and take up their cross and follow me” (Matthew 16:24 NIV), He reveals that the path He walks will also become the path of His followers.

The Transfiguration provides a glimpse of the glory that lies beyond the suffering. For a brief moment, Peter, James, and John see Jesus revealed in heavenly splendor and hear the Father’s voice affirm that He is the beloved Son. The vision assures them that the suffering ahead does not diminish His glory. The King who walks toward Jerusalem remains the King of heaven.

As Jesus continues His journey, much of His teaching focuses on the character of those who belong to His Kingdom. Through parables and personal instruction, He challenges His disciples to embrace humility, forgiveness, faithfulness, and servant-hearted leadership. The question is no longer simply whether they recognize the King, but whether they are willing to allow Him to shape their lives.

The section reaches its climax with Jesus’ declaration that “the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.” (Matthew 20:28 NIV) These words provide the key to understanding everything that follows. The King is indeed going to Jerusalem, but He is going there not to claim an earthly throne. He is going to give Himself for His people. That journey now reaches its decisive stage as Jesus enters Jerusalem for the final week of His earthly ministry.

The Rejected King in His Royal City (Matthew 21-23)
#

As Jesus enters Jerusalem, the atmosphere is filled with expectation. The crowds welcome Him as the Son of David, celebrating the arrival of God’s promised King. Yet Matthew quickly reveals that this triumphal entry is also a judicial entry. The King has come to His city, but He comes not only to save but also to examine and judge.

Since Caesarea Philippi, Jesus has been preparing His disciples for this moment. His teaching has increasingly focused on the deeper issues of the heart, challenging people not merely to admire the King but to submit to His authority. The question is no longer whether Jesus possesses power, but whether people are willing to allow Him to shape their lives and character.

This tension comes to a climax in Jerusalem. Entering the temple, Jesus drives out the money changers and confronts the corruption that has overtaken Israel’s worship. What follows is a series of increasingly sharp confrontations with the religious leaders, who repeatedly challenge His authority while refusing to recognize the One standing before them.

Matthew’s account reaches its peak in Jesus’ final public teaching. Earlier in the Gospel, the dominant note was blessing. Now another word repeatedly appears: “Woe.” Again and again, Jesus declares, “Woe to you, teachers of the law and Pharisees, you hypocrites!” (Matthew 23:13 NIV). The repeated warnings reveal both His sorrow and His judgment upon leaders who knew the Scriptures yet failed to receive their King.

The tragedy is not a lack of religious activity but a failure of the heart. The leaders possessed knowledge, influence, and outward righteousness, yet remained unwilling to submit themselves to God’s rule. Matthew therefore turns the question toward every reader. It is possible to know much about God while resisting His authority. The challenge is not merely to recognize the King, but to welcome His reign within our lives.

As Jesus laments over Jerusalem, the joy of the triumphal entry gives way to the sadness of rejection. The King has come to His people, yet many refuse to receive Him. The shadow of the cross begins to fall across the city, and the final stage of His mission draws near.

The King’s Final Teaching and the Coming Kingdom (Matthew 24-25)
#

As Jesus leaves the temple, the disciples draw His attention to its magnificent buildings. Their confidence in what seems permanent and secure is immediately challenged when Jesus predicts its destruction. This leads to one of the longest teaching sections in Matthew’s Gospel, often called the Olivet Discourse.

Having pronounced judgment upon the nation, Jesus now turns His attention once more to His disciples. Looking beyond His death and resurrection, He prepares them for the years that will follow before His return. He speaks of deception, persecution, spiritual conflict, and coming judgment, revealing that the path ahead will not be easy. The powers of darkness will continue to oppose God’s people, and their faith will be tested in ways that expose the true foundations of their lives.

Yet the purpose of this teaching is not to promote fear but faithfulness. Rather than focusing on dates or predictions, Jesus emphasizes perseverance. His followers must learn to trust God amid uncertainty, drawing strength from Him as they await the fulfillment of His promises.

This theme runs through the parables that follow. The faithful servant, the wise virgins, and the servants entrusted with talents all illustrate the same truth: discipleship requires watchfulness, readiness, and steadfast obedience. The King’s followers are called not merely to begin well but to remain faithful until He comes.

For readers today, the message remains the same. The future belongs to God, and the King will return. Until then, His people are called to live with confidence, faithfulness, and hope, trusting the One who rules history and will one day bring His Kingdom in its fullness.

The King’s Victory Through Sacrifice (Matthew 26-27)
#

As Matthew moves into the final chapters of his Gospel, the story reaches the climax toward which everything has been moving. The betrayal, the trials, the agony of Gethsemane, and the crucifixion are not tragic interruptions to Jesus’ mission but its fulfillment.

During the Last Supper, Jesus reveals the meaning of His approaching death: “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.” (Matthew 26:28 NIV) What follows is therefore no accident of history. Jesus willingly gives Himself to accomplish the salvation of His people.

From the garden of Gethsemane to the judgment hall, from the whipping post to Golgotha, Jesus moves steadily toward the cross. Betrayed by Judas, abandoned by His disciples, condemned by religious leaders, and sentenced by Pilate, He appears to stand alone. Yet beneath these events, a far greater conflict is taking place. In the mystery of the cross, Jesus confronts the powers of sin, evil, and death that have enslaved humanity and wins the victory through His obedience and sacrifice.

The irony of Matthew’s account is striking. The only crown Jesus wears is a crown of thorns. The only throne He ascends is a cross. The only sceptre placed in His hand is a reed used to mock Him. Yet it is precisely through this apparent humiliation that the King achieves His greatest triumph.

What appears to be defeat is in fact victory. The darkness covering the land, the tearing of the temple curtain, and the confession of the centurion all point to the significance of what has happened. At the cross, God’s justice and mercy meet, sin is judged, and the power of death is broken. The King accomplishes exactly what He came to do, securing redemption for His people and triumphing over the forces that stood against them.

The Risen King and the Mission of His People (Matthew 28)
#

Had the Gospel ended with the burial of Jesus, it would have been a story of profound tragedy. Instead, Matthew brings his readers to the empty tomb and the announcement that changed history: “He is not here; he has risen, just as he said.” (Matthew 28:6 NIV)

The resurrection confirms everything Jesus claimed about Himself. The Messiah rejected by men has been vindicated by God. The King who appeared defeated has conquered sin, death, and the grave.

Yet Matthew points beyond the fact of the resurrection to its significance. Through His death and resurrection, Jesus opens the way for God’s presence to dwell with His people in a new and deeper way. The risen Christ is not merely a King to be admired from a distance. He is the living Lord who comes to reign within the lives of those who trust Him. The Kingdom of Heaven becomes a present reality wherever the King is welcomed and obeyed.

Having triumphed over death, Jesus commissions His disciples to make disciples of all nations, extending the message of the Kingdom throughout the world. Matthew’s Gospel ends where it began. The child introduced as Immanuel, “God with us,” is now the risen King who promises: “And surely I am with you always, to the very end of the age.” (Matthew 28:20 NIV)

The King who came near remains with His people still, ruling not only from heaven but also within the hearts of those who belong to Him.

So What? Why Does Matthew Part 2 Matter Today?
#

The second half of Matthew’s Gospel reveals that recognizing Jesus as the King is only the beginning. The disciples confessed that He was the Messiah long before they fully understood the kind of King He had come to be. Like them, we are often drawn to power, success, and visible triumph. Yet Matthew presents a King whose greatest victory comes through humility, suffering, and sacrifice.

As the Gospel moves toward Jerusalem, the cross becomes the centre of the story. What appears to be defeat is revealed as God’s triumph over sin, death, and the powers of darkness. Through His death and resurrection, Jesus not only secures forgiveness for His people but also opens the way for God’s presence to dwell within them. The risen Christ continues to reign within the lives of those who trust Him.

This brings Matthew’s readers to the central question of the Gospel. It is not simply whether Jesus is the Messiah, nor merely whether we believe that He died and rose again. The deeper question is whether He has become King of our lives. Has He gained our trust, our loyalty, and our worship? Has His rule reached the deepest part of our hearts?

Matthew begins with the announcement that the Kingdom of Heaven has drawn near and ends with the promise of the King’s continuing presence. Wherever Christ is welcomed and obeyed, the Kingdom is already at work. The One introduced as Immanuel, “God with us,” remains with His people still. The question Matthew leaves before every reader is therefore both simple and searching: Is Jesus Christ truly King of your life?

For it is only when the King is enthroned in the heart that the Kingdom is present in its fullness.

Closing Story: Following the King Home
#

Imagine a group of travellers searching for a homeland they have never seen but have long believed exists. The journey is often difficult. Some roads appear promising but lead nowhere. Other paths offer comfort but fail to satisfy the deeper longing that keeps drawing the travellers onward. Along the way, they encounter many voices offering easier routes, yet none seem capable of leading them where they truly wish to go.

Then they meet a guide unlike any other. What makes him different is not merely that he knows the road, but that he has already travelled it himself. He knows every danger, every steep climb, and every difficult passage because he has walked the path before them. More than that, he bears the marks of the journey upon himself. At one point, the road disappears into a dark mountain pass. Many hesitate. The way ahead seems uncertain, and some wonder whether another route might be easier. But the guide explains that beyond the darkness lies the homeland they have been seeking all along. He knows because he has already passed through it and returned to lead others safely home. Suddenly the most important question is no longer how difficult the road may be. The question is whether the guide can be trusted.

As Matthew’s Gospel comes to its conclusion, readers find themselves facing a similar decision. The King has been revealed. His teachings have been heard. His compassion has been witnessed. His authority has been demonstrated. He has walked the road to Jerusalem, endured the cross, entered the darkness of death, and emerged victorious in the resurrection. Now He invites others to follow Him.

The Gospel closes with a promise rather than an ending. The King who was introduced as Immanuel, “God with us,” remains with His people still. His final words continue to echo through every generation: “And surely I am with you always, to the very end of the age.” (Matthew 28:20 NIV)

The journey continues, but we do not travel alone. The King who conquered the cross and the grave has gone before us, and He walks with His people still until the day they reach their true home.
